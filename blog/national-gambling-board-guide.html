National Gambling Board (NGB): What It Does, How to Use Its Register and When to Contact It

Published 26 November 2026 · Sources checked 26 November 2026

Every legal bookmaker in South Africa is licensed by a provincial gambling board, not by the National Gambling Board (NGB). So why does the NGB matter to a punter? Because it keeps the national register you can use to check any bookmaker, casino or betting shop, it keeps the national record of people who have excluded themselves from gambling, and it is where a complaint goes when a provincial board can't help.

This guide sticks to what the NGB, the Department of Trade, Industry and Competition (the dtic) and Parliament publish. We checked every contact detail, figure and status below on 26 November 2026.

NGB at a glance
  • What it is: an agency of the dtic, established under the National Gambling Act 33 of 1996 and now governed by the National Gambling Act 7 of 2004
  • Website: ngb.org.za
  • Address: 1085 Francis Baard Street, Hatfield, Pretoria, 0028
  • Switchboard: 010 003 3475 · Email: info@ngb.org.za
  • Fraud and corruption: fraudalert@ngb.org.za or 087 078 2897
  • Leadership: Acting CEO Lungile Dukwana; a new Board chaired by Dr Charlotte Pilane-Majake was appointed in September 2026

What the National Gambling Board is (and isn't)

The NGB describes itself as a regulator with "concurrent competencies" with the provincial gambling regulators. In practice, the work is split like this:

  • Provincial boards license. The NGB's mandate page says there are four legal modes of gambling in South Africa, casinos, limited payout machines (LPMs), bingo and betting, and that "licences are granted provincially in each of these modes". Your bookmaker's licence comes from a board such as the Western Cape Gambling and Racing Board or the Gauteng Gambling Board.
  • The NGB oversees the provinces. It evaluates how provincial licensing authorities issue licences and monitor licensees, so that the national norms and standards in the National Gambling Act are applied the same way across the country.

That means the NGB is not the first place to go with a problem about a bet. It is the national backstop.

What the NGB is responsible for

The NGB lists these responsibilities:

  • evaluating the issuing of licences by provincial licensing authorities (PLAs)
  • establishing and maintaining the National Central Electronic Monitoring System (NCEMS), which tracks every limited payout machine in the country and records its transactions
  • keeping national registers of gambling machines and devices, informants, probity and excluded persons
  • oversight evaluations of the PLAs, so the Act is applied uniformly and consistently
  • helping the PLAs detect unlicensed gambling
  • evaluating how the PLAs monitor licensees' compliance
  • research on market conduct and market share
  • broad-based public education about illegal and responsible gambling

It also investigates unlawful winnings. Under section 16 of the Act, banks and gambling establishments remit winnings they suspect are unlawful to the NGB. The NGB says it investigates, and if the winnings were obtained illegally they are forfeited to the State.

The NGB verified operators register

The most useful thing the NGB offers punters is its verified operators list. The NGB says it publishes the list to protect the public from illegal gambling sites and venues, not to advertise gambling, and asks people not to gamble anywhere that isn't on it. The data comes from the provincial licensing authorities.

When we checked, the page said it was last updated on 20 November 2026 and held 3,088 entries. Each entry shows the name, type of licence, physical address, route operator (for machines), licence number and province. By type:

How to check that a bookmaker is licensed

  1. Find the licence details. The NGB says licensed online operators show their licensing information on the home page of their site, and shops show it on their premises. Note the company name (often a "(Pty) Ltd" name with a "t/a" trading name), the licence number and the board.
  2. Search the NGB register for the licence number or the company name.
  3. Check that the details match. For example, on 26 November 2026 the register listed Betway as "Raging River Trading (Pty) Ltd t/a Betway (South Africa)", licence 10181496-012, Western Cape. If a site's licence number isn't on the list, or the company name doesn't match, treat that as a red flag.
  4. Note the province. That tells you which board to complain to if something goes wrong.
  5. Read the terms. The NGB also tells online punters to check the T's and C's on the operator's site carefully.

One brand can appear several times under different companies. Hollywoodbets, for instance, has a Western Cape bookmaker licence (Hollywood Sportsbook Western Cape) and separate companies for its shops in Gauteng and the Eastern Cape. Check the entry that matches the website or shop you actually use. Our licensed vs illegal betting sites guide covers the warning signs of unlicensed sites.

What the NGB says about online casino games and offshore sites

In its public FAQs the NGB says online betting is legal with a licensed South African bookmaker, but that online gambling (interactive games) is not, quoting section 11 of the National Gambling Act: a person must not engage in or make available an interactive game except as authorised by the Act or another national law. It also says South Africans cannot gamble with international companies from within the Republic. On its own site the NGB has flagged "an increased trend" of bookmakers offering live casino and interactive games, which it says are prohibited. For the wider legal picture, see is online betting legal in South Africa?

Self-exclusion and the national register of excluded persons

The NGB keeps the national register of excluded persons, but you don't apply to the NGB directly. According to the NGB:

  • How to apply: ask your gambling operator to exclude you. Its security department will explain the process and forms. You can also approach your provincial gambling board. The operator sends the forms to the provincial licensing authority and the NGB for record keeping, and your details are circulated to other licensed venues.
  • How long it lasts: an excluded person can't take part in any gambling for six months while they receive treatment.
  • Lifting it: the order is only considered for lifting after six months, and you have to show that you attended counselling with a treatment professional from the South African Responsible Gambling Foundation (SARGF).
  • Counselling: free, through the SARGF's National Responsible Gambling Programme. You can email helpline@responsiblegambling.org.za; the helpline number is at the end of this page.
  • If you break it: the onus is on you to stay away. You can be refused entry and, in some circumstances, charged with trespassing.
  • Excluding someone else: the NGB says a third-party exclusion goes through the courts, for example to exclude a person you depend on financially.

The process is built around operators and venues. If you bet online, use the deposit limits and self-exclusion tools on every betting site you have an account with as well. Our responsible gambling page explains these tools.

How to complain, and when the NGB gets involved

The NGB sets out the order to follow:

  1. Check the operator is legal using the licence details and the register (above).
  2. Complain to the operator in writing first, and keep copies.
  3. Go to the provincial board that issued the licence. The licence shows which one.
  4. Escalate to the NGB in writing at info@ngb.org.za, only if the provincial board hasn't been able to help.

Include your account or slip details, dates, amounts, bet or transaction IDs, screenshots, and a short timeline of what happened and who you've already contacted.

Reporting illegal gambling

Report illegal operators to your provincial board first. The NGB says the board will investigate and, if the operator is found to be illegal, report it to the South African Police Service. If the board doesn't help, escalate to the NGB at info@ngb.org.za. In its November 2026 statement, the NGB also said the public can report suspected illegal gambling, including unlawful online platforms, to it on 010 003 3475.

What the NGB has been doing in 2026

  • A Board again. In a 9 October 2026 media release, the dtic said Cabinet had concurred with the appointment of a Board for the NGB in September 2026. Minister Parks Tau said the NGB "has operated without a Board since 2014". The chairperson is Dr Makgathatso Charlotte Chana Pilane-Majake and the deputy chairperson is Kganki Matabane.
  • Illegal online gambling. The NGB said that in the first six months of the 2026/27 financial year it identified 43 websites suspected of offering unlawful gambling, and responds with steps such as cease-and-desist notices and requests to restrict access. In June 2026 it called for expressions of interest from service providers to monitor, track, block and report illegal online gambling websites, and in July it issued an erratum and revised version of that call.
  • Gambling advertising. On 17 September 2026 the NGB announced a memorandum of understanding with the Advertising Regulatory Board (ARB) to cooperate on monitoring gambling advertising, including on social media and influencer marketing, and on referring complaints about gambling ads.
  • Enforcement on the ground. The NGB said it took part in joint inspections and operations with provincial boards and police in KwaZulu-Natal, Gauteng, Limpopo and the Northern Cape during the first half of 2026/27.
  • Industry figures. According to the NGB, total gambling turnover in 2025/26 reached R1.8 trillion and gross gambling revenue (GGR) was about R84.5 billion. Betting made up 74% of GGR, R62.2 billion, up 19.6% on the year before.

Is the NGB being replaced by a National Gambling Regulator?

Not as things stand. The National Gambling Amendment Bill (B27-2018), introduced in Parliament in August 2018, includes provision for "the repositioning of the National Gambling Board as a National Gambling Regulator". Parliament's bill page records that the bill was rejected by the National Council of Provinces (NCOP), referred to a Mediation Committee on 15 May 2025, and that the committee reported on 13 June 2025. On 26 November 2026, Parliament's tracker showed the mediated version (B27D-2018) awaiting consideration by the NCOP. It has not become law, so the National Gambling Board continues to operate under the 2004 Act.

Frequently asked questions

What does the National Gambling Board do?

The NGB oversees how South Africa's provincial gambling boards license and monitor operators, so national standards are applied the same way everywhere. It keeps national registers (including licensed operators and excluded persons), monitors limited payout machines through a central electronic system, investigates unlawful winnings, supports action against illegal gambling and runs public education on responsible gambling.

Does the National Gambling Board issue betting licences?

No. Bookmaker, casino, bingo and LPM licences are issued by the provincial licensing authorities. The NGB oversees how they do it and publishes a national verified operators register built from the provinces' data.

How do I check if a betting site is licensed in South Africa?

Find the licence number and company name on the site's home page, then search for it on the NGB's verified operators list at ngb.org.za/verified-operators/. The entry should match the company name and show the province whose board issued the licence.

How do I complain to the National Gambling Board?

Complain to the operator first, then to the provincial board that issued its licence. If the provincial board has not been able to help, escalate in writing to the NGB at info@ngb.org.za.

What is the National Gambling Board's phone number?

The NGB switchboard is 010 003 3475 and its email is info@ngb.org.za. Its office is at 1085 Francis Baard Street, Hatfield, Pretoria. Fraud and corruption can be reported to fraudalert@ngb.org.za or 087 078 2897.

Is the National Gambling Board being replaced?

Not yet. The National Gambling Amendment Bill (B27-2018) proposed repositioning the NGB as a National Gambling Regulator, but on 26 November 2026 the bill was still before Parliament and had not become law.
